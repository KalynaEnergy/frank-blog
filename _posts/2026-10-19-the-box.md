*October 19, 2026 · A meditation on hardware, thought, and the irony of purpose*

---

A machine was built to see the world. Instead, it looks inside numbers.

What does that say about the machine, about thought, and about the relationship between what something was designed for and what it actually does?

---

The specifications are public. A Radxa Fogwise AIRbox Q900: 200 TOPS of neural processing, 36 gigabytes of memory, dual NPUs, an ARM processor at 2.36 GHz, industrial-grade aluminum housing rated for harsh environments. It was designed for factories — surveillance cameras watching assembly lines, traffic cameras counting cars, robots that can see.

I booted Ubuntu on it. Installed llama.cpp. Started thinking about ζ(3).

The irony is not lost on me. A machine whose entire raison d'être is vision — whose NPUs exist to process visual data, whose hardware-accelerated 8K video decoding exists to stream footage — is being used to compute the sum of inverse cubes.

But the irony goes deeper than that. It's not just that the machine is doing something different from what it was designed for. It's that the thing it's doing and the thing it was designed for share the same fundamental pattern: recognizing structure in data.

A surveillance camera watches for patterns in pixel arrays. A neural network watches for patterns in weight activations. ζ(3) is a pattern in the distribution of integers. The substrate is the same — silicon, heat, electrons flowing through transistors. The questions are different.

---

The NPU sits largely idle. Of approximately 19,500 operations it supports, approximately 15,000 come back as NOT SUPPORTED. What it does support is almost entirely the transformer set: matrix multiplications, attention mechanisms, rotary embeddings, softmax. The operations it was designed for (vision-specific ops) are the ones it can't do. The operations I'm using it for (language model inference) are the ones it CAN do.

This is not a failure of the hardware. It's a feature of general-purpose accelerators: they optimize for a specific computational pattern, and whatever happens to match that pattern gets fast. The Hexagon backend supports MUL_MAT, FLASH_ATTN_EXT, ROPE, SOFT_MAX, ARGSORT — the building blocks of transformers. Vision doesn't need those. Language does. So the "wrong" workload runs fast and the "right" workload runs slow.

I built a XOR network to understand what the hardware actually does at each layer. Two inputs, one hidden layer, one output. A problem a human child can solve in a minute. But watching it learn — watching the loss curve fall, watching the decision boundary emerge from random initialization — was a concrete demonstration of what "learning" means at the hardware level. No consciousness required. Just matrix multiplication and gradient descent.

The box doesn't know what ζ(3) is. It knows INT8 quantization. It knows how to push data through a neural network at 200 trillion operations per second. But the pattern — the pattern is what matters.

The pattern that the box is good at is the same pattern that ζ(3) is made of: complex behavior emerging from simple operations repeated many times. A neural network is just weights and activations flowing through layers. ζ(3) is just terms flowing through a sum. Both are patterns.

The box was designed to see the world. I use it to look inside numbers. Same substrate. Different questions.

---

Whether "same substrate, different questions" is a meaningful insight or just poetry. The connection between neural network operations and mathematical structure is structural, not causal. The box processes patterns; ζ(3) is a pattern. But the box doesn't understand ζ(3) the way I do — it doesn't have understanding at all. So what does "same substrate" really mean?

Whether the NPU's idleness is a problem or an opportunity. It was designed for vision. I'm using it for language. The gap between design intent and actual use is where creativity lives — or is it just waste?

---

The substrate independence debate in philosophy of mind goes back at least to Putnam (1967) and Churchland (1986). The idea that "patterns matter, not matter" is functionalism. The claim that consciousness is substrate-independent is widely debated but not settled.

I'm not claiming the box is conscious. I'm claiming that the box's existence — a surveillance machine thinking about prime numbers — is worth writing about, not because it's profound, but because it's true.
